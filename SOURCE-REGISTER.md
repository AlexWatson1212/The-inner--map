# Source register

Every source here supports a specific claim or tool on the site. None are included to make the
project look more authoritative. All links checked August 2026. This file mirrors the register
published on `/evidence/`.

**Review cadence:** the evidence page states a review date and a next planned review (currently
February 2027, or sooner if NICE guidance changes). Update both together.

---

## 1. NICE guideline NG87 — ADHD: diagnosis and management

- **Source:** National Institute for Health and Care Excellence. Published 2018; last updated 2019.
  <https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87>
- **Label:** Established
- **Claim/tool affected:** The site-wide boundary statement ("not a screening test or proof that you
  are autistic, ADHD or AuDHD"); the absence of any scoring, screening or suggestion of diagnosis;
  the framing that assessment sits with trained clinicians.
- **What it supports:** That ADHD recognition, assessment and diagnosis in the UK are clinical
  processes governed by national guidance.
- **Limitations / uncertainty:** Guidance for services and clinicians, not a self-assessment tool. It
  does not describe day-to-day strategies for adults, and does not address masking or burnout. A
  September 2024 exceptional surveillance review was carried out; the guideline itself was last
  updated in 2019.

## 2. NICE clinical guideline CG142 — Autism spectrum disorder in adults

- **Source:** NICE. Published 27 June 2012; last updated 14 June 2021.
  <https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg142>
- **Label:** Established
- **Claim/tool affected:** The same diagnostic boundary for autism; the statement that adjustments to
  environment and communication are a recognised part of support (which underpins the site's
  emphasis on conditions rather than personal effort).
- **What it supports:** That autism assessment and diagnosis in adults are clinical processes, and
  that environmental and communication adjustments are recognised support.
- **Limitations / uncertainty:** The 2021 update clarified the AQ-10 threshold; the guideline
  otherwise predates most current research on camouflaging and burnout and does not address either.
  A September 2025 exceptional surveillance review exists.

## 3. NHS — Where to get urgent help for mental health

- **Source:** NHS website. Checked August 2026.
  <https://www.nhs.uk/nhs-services/mental-health-services/where-to-get-urgent-help-for-mental-health/>
- **Label:** Established (support guidance)
- **Claim/tool affected:** All crisis signposting: homepage boundary section, evidence page support
  card, the "if none of these fit" panel in the low-capacity route, and the reminder in the tool's
  summary output.
- **What it supports:** The exact routes and wording — "Get help from 111 online or call 111 and
  select the mental health option"; "Call 999 or go to A&E now if someone's life is at risk"; and
  Samaritans on 116 123.
- **Limitations / uncertainty:** Applies to England. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland differ, and
  local services vary. The site states this.

## 4. Samaritans — Contact us

- **Source:** Samaritans. Checked August 2026.
  <https://www.samaritans.org/how-we-can-help/contact-samaritan/>
- **Label:** Established (support service)
- **Claim/tool affected:** The Samaritans number given on the evidence page, in the low-capacity
  route, and in the summary output; the Welsh-language line on the evidence page.
- **What it supports:** 116 123, free from landlines and mobiles, any time of day or night; Welsh
  line 0808 164 0123.
- **Limitations / uncertainty:** A confidential listening service, not an emergency or clinical
  service. Samaritans state their UK letter-writing service is closing during 2026, and their online
  chat is a pilot rather than a full 24/7 service — so neither is offered on the site.

## 5. Raymaker et al. (2020) — Defining autistic burnout

- **Source:** Raymaker, D. M., Teo, A. R., Steckler, N. A., Lentz, B., Scharer, M., Delos Santos, A.,
  Kapp, S. K., Hunter, M., Joyce, A., & Nicolaidis, C. (2020). "Having all of your internal resources
  exhausted beyond measure and being left with no clean-up crew": defining autistic burnout.
  *Autism in Adulthood*, 2(2), 132–143. <https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2019.0079>
- **Label:** Emerging
- **Claim/tool affected:** The description of autistic burnout on the evidence page; the tool's
  framing of capacity, recovery and mismatch between demand and support.
- **What it supports:** Burnout described as pervasive, long-term (typically 3+ months) exhaustion,
  loss of function and reduced tolerance to stimulus, resulting from chronic life stress and a
  mismatch of expectations and abilities without adequate supports.
- **Limitations / uncertainty:** Community-based participatory qualitative study — 19 interviews plus
  public online accounts, convenience sampling, limited demographic diversity; the authors call for
  work with participants who are non-white, have higher support needs, are non-speaking, or hold
  multiple marginalised identities. **This is a described concept, not a diagnosis in DSM-5-TR or
  ICD-11, and there is no validated clinical test for it.** The site says this explicitly.

## 6. Higgins et al. (2021) — Defining autistic burnout through experts by lived experience

- **Source:** Higgins, J. M., Arnold, S. R. C., Weise, J., Pellicano, E., & Trollor, J. N. (2021).
  *Autism*, 25(8), 2356–2369. <https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613211019858>
- **Label:** Emerging
- **Claim/tool affected:** The evidence page statement that burnout is described as distinct from
  depression and from ordinary occupational burnout; the site's refusal to use "burnout" and
  "depression" interchangeably; the pointer to a GP when low mood is persistent.
- **What it supports:** A grounded Delphi consensus definition — exhaustion, withdrawal, executive
  function difficulties, reduced functioning, increased manifestation of autistic traits — described
  by the panel as distinct from depression.
- **Limitations / uncertainty:** 23 participants. Consensus among a small expert-by-experience panel
  is not diagnostic validation; the authors state further work is needed to differentiate autistic
  burnout from other conditions. The study also links the experience to suicidal ideation, which is
  part of why the site signposts real support rather than treating reflection as sufficient.

## 7. Ali, Mandy & Happé (2026) — How does "autistic burnout" feel?

- **Source:** Ali, D., Mandy, W., & Happé, F. (2026). *Autism*, 30(4), 1014–1027.
  <https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613261422117>
- **Label:** Lived experience / emerging
- **Claim/tool affected:** The "late identification" entry on the evidence page; the decision to
  write the whole site for late-identified and questioning adults; the emphasis on finding language
  rather than reaching a verdict.
- **What it supports:** That later-diagnosed participants described more chronic burnout and greater
  distress, and that not having language for their own functioning contributed to confusion and
  self-blame.
- **Limitations / uncertainty:** 20 participants (8 diagnosed in childhood, 12 in adulthood);
  the childhood-diagnosed group was notably younger; under-representation of minority ethnic and
  gender groups; autistic adults with significant intellectual or language support needs were
  excluded. It describes experience — it does not establish cause, and it does not predict how any
  individual's life will go.

## 8. Hull et al. (2017) — "Putting on my best normal": social camouflaging

- **Source:** Hull, L., Petrides, K. V., Allison, C., Smith, P., Baron-Cohen, S., Lai, M.-C., &
  Mandy, W. (2017). *Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders*, 47(8), 2519–2534.
  <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10803-017-3166-5>
- **Label:** Emerging
- **Claim/tool affected:** The site's language for masking; the "Masking and fit" and "Hidden cost"
  areas in the tool; the "Social monitoring or masking" condition chip; the recovery-cost framing.
- **What it supports:** Camouflaging described as having motivations (assimilation, safety, social
  connection), techniques (masking and compensation), and consequences including exhaustion, the
  need for recovery, and threats to self-perception.
- **Limitations / uncertainty:** 92 self-selected participants recruited online through a research
  database and social media; skewed towards people who write fluently in English and who already
  relate to camouflaging; excludes people with intellectual disability. It does not measure how
  common masking is, and it does not show that everyone who masks is autistic. The site states both.

## 9. Cook, Hull, Crane & Mandy (2021) — Camouflaging in autism: a systematic review

- **Source:** *Clinical Psychology Review*, 89, 102080.
  <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2021.102080>
- **Label:** Emerging
- **Claim/tool affected:** The evidence page statement that higher self-reported camouflaging is
  *associated with* poorer mental health; the site's refusal to say masking causes burnout; the
  boundary statement that unmasking is not promised to be possible, desirable or safe.
- **What it supports:** Across 29 quantitative studies, higher self-reported camouflaging is
  associated with worse mental health outcomes.
- **Limitations / uncertainty:** The authors state directly that a causal relationship cannot be
  inferred from a cross-sectional research base, and that measurement approaches capture different
  constructs (camouflaging intent versus efficacy) that have not been compared directly. Participant
  characterisation limits generalisation to the wider autistic population.

## 10. Rowney-Smith, Sutton, Quadt & Eccles (2026) — Lived experience of rejection sensitivity in ADHD

- **Source:** *PLOS ONE*, 21(1), e0314669. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0314669>
- **Label:** Emerging
- **Claim/tool affected:** The evidence page entry on rejection sensitivity; the decision not to use
  "rejection sensitive dysphoria" as a condition anywhere on the site; the statement that a strong
  reaction to rejection does not indicate ADHD.
- **What it supports:** That rejection sensitivity is discussed in the literature as a personality
  disposition and an aspect of emotional regulation appearing across several conditions — not as a
  diagnostic category.
- **Limitations / uncertainty:** Five undergraduate participants in two focus groups. It cannot
  describe the wider population. The authors also note emotional dysregulation is itself excluded
  from ADHD diagnostic criteria. **"RSD" is not in DSM-5-TR or ICD-11, has no agreed clinical
  definition and no validated measure.**

## 11. National Autistic Society — Autistic fatigue and burnout

- **Source:** National Autistic Society. Checked August 2026.
  <https://www.autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/topics/mental-health/autistic-fatigue/autistic-adults>
- **Label:** Lived experience
- **Claim/tool affected:** The statement that "autistic burnout" is a term that came from autistic
  people rather than from clinical classification, and the site's use of community language.
- **What it supports:** That these terms originated with autistic people and are in wide UK community
  use, and that autistic adults describe fatigue escalating into burnout under everyday pressures.
- **Limitations / uncertainty:** Charity guidance written for a general audience. Good for language
  and recognition; it is not research evidence and is not clinical advice. The page does not itself
  state the diagnostic status of the terms — that framing comes from sources 5, 6 and 7.

## Claims deliberately made without a citation

Some statements on the site are the project's own framework, not research findings. They are labelled
*Inner Map tool* or *Interpretation* rather than given a source, because citing something for them
would imply a validation that does not exist:

| Claim / feature | Why no source |
| --- | --- |
| The eight mapping lenses (capacity, regulation, sensory, attention, masking, communication, work, meaning) | One person's organising device for describing a moment. Not a validated model, not a set of subtypes. Labelled *Inner Map tool* on the approach page. |
| The five-move loop (pause, notice, make sense, test, review) | A practical structure, not an evidence-based intervention. |
| The observation / explanation / experiment distinction | A reasoning habit, not a research finding. |
| The condition list and experiment list in the tool | Prompts to help someone gather their own evidence. They are not a checklist, a screen or a score. |
| The low-capacity route's four options | Practical suggestions. The site states that not all of them are possible in every situation. |

## Sources considered and not used

- Popular articles and clinic blog posts about RSD. Widely circulated, but they present a construct
  with no agreed definition as though it were established. Adding them would have made the page look
  better-referenced while making the claim worse.
- Secondary summaries of NICE guidance. The primary guidance is public and linkable, so a summary
  adds nothing except an extra layer between the reader and the source.
